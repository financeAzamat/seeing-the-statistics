UDJ · Module 1 — Heuristics and Biases

Six questions you will probably get wrong

Not because you are careless. Because the shortcuts are usually right, and these are the cases where they are not.

Every other page in this course shows you a statistical idea. This one shows you your own judgment. Answer the six questions on the right before reading the explanations. Commit to an answer, because an intuition you never stated is an intuition you can always claim you never had.

Why guessing badly is the point

Your mind does not compute. It substitutes. Asked something hard — what is the typical order value across twenty thousand invoices — it quietly answers something easier, like what does a middling order look like to me, and hands you that instead. The substitution is invisible and it usually works. Fast, cheap, right often enough.

The whole of statistics is a set of tools for the cases where it does not. So the useful thing is not to be told that biases exist. It is to feel one land, on real data, with a number attached to how far off you were.

hard question → an easier one gets answered the answer still feels like yours · that feeling is the bias

What this page does and does not claim

An honest warning about method. This page makes no claim about what other people guess. There is no survey behind it, and inventing one would break the rule the rest of the course runs on. It only ever shows your own answer against the measured one.

Every correct answer comes from data already measured elsewhere in this course — 19,773 real invoices, 53,940 real diamonds, 272 real eruptions — and each question names the module where that measurement is worked through. If a figure here disagreed with the page it came from, the build would fail rather than ship.

The four shortcuts these questions catch

  • The average is the typical case. True for symmetric data. Money data is almost never symmetric, and then the average sits above most of the crowd.
  • A single number describes a whole distribution. A mean, a correlation, an accuracy — each is one projection of something with a shape, and the shape is where the decisions live.
  • A statistic is a property of the world. Most are properties of your sample. Change which rows you can see and the number moves without anything in the world changing.
  • A rule of thumb travels without its conditions. "n = 30" is remembered; the assumption it depends on is not.

How to read your scorecard

The middle panel keeps every answer you give and marks where the truth was. What matters is not the tally but the direction and size of the misses. A pattern of guesses that all sit on the same side of the truth is a systematic bias, not bad luck — and a systematic bias is the kind you can correct for once you know it is there.

The point of a bias is that knowing about it does not switch it off. You correct for it with procedure — look at the distribution, ask what a useless model would score, check the range of your sample — not with resolve.

Where this appears in practice

  • In forecasting, anchoring on last year's number and adjusting is the substitution in its purest form, and the adjustment is reliably too small.
  • On a dashboard, a single headline metric is exactly the object your mind is happiest to substitute for the thing you care about.
  • In a post-mortem, once you know an outcome the path to it looks inevitable, so the decision gets judged by the result rather than by what was knowable at the time.
  • When sampling, a small sample that agrees with your expectation feels like confirmation. It is usually just a small sample.

In exam language

A heuristic is a judgment shortcut that substitutes an accessible attribute for the target attribute; a bias is the systematic error that substitution produces. The classical programme is Tversky and Kahneman (1974), which named representativeness, availability and anchoring and adjustment.

Questions 1 and 2 exercise the failure to reason from a distribution's shape rather than a point summary. Question 5 is insensitivity to sample size — the "law of small numbers" — in the form where a rule of thumb is retained and its regularity condition discarded.

Questions 3 and 4 exercise range restriction and its mirror, aggregation across a mixture: both are cases where a statistic is a property of the sampling frame rather than of the population. Question 6 is base-rate neglect, in the form where a skewed base rate makes an uninformative classifier look accurate.

Note what is deliberately absent. Demonstrating anchoring or availability properly needs a between-subjects manipulation, which a single reader cannot supply, so this page does not attempt to measure those and does not report a population effect for any bias.
