UDJ · Module 7 — Regression & R²

Fitting a line, and what the fit is worth

R² is the most quoted number in analytics and the most misread.

A regression is one sentence: give me an x and I will give you back a y. Give me the size of a diamond, I will give you a price. The line is the sentence. R² is a claim about how much you should trust it — and on this page you can watch that claim be wrong twice.

Drag the line yourself

The line has a handle at each end. Drag either one (or use the two sliders, if you prefer a keyboard). Every point gets a thin vertical thread to the line — that is the error for that point, the amount your line missed it by.

Underneath, one number adds all those errors up. Try to make it as small as you can, then press best fit — the exact answer, solved rather than nudged. You cannot beat it. That is what "least squares" means: of all the lines that could be drawn, exactly one makes that total smallest.

Why squares, and not just distances

Each error is squared before being added. Two reasons, both practical: squaring stops a miss of +50 cancelling a miss of −50, and it punishes one big miss far harder than several small ones. Turn on squares and you see it literally — each error drawn as a square whose area is what gets added up.

What R² actually is

Start with a question: how wrong would you be if you had no line at all and just guessed the average every time? That is the gold dashed line, and the errors from it are the total variation to be explained.

R² = the share of that variation your line got rid of

The bar across the middle of the chart is exactly this: blue for what the line explained, red for what is left over. R² is the blue share. Drag the line badly enough and R² goes negative — which simply means your line is worse than having no line and using the average.

Now the part that matters

Of the three datasets here, the two with the highest R² are the two you should trust least. R² cannot see what is wrong with them. The bottom panel can.

The bottom panel is how you check a fit

It plots the leftover error against x. If a line is a fair description of the data, this panel should look like featureless noise — an even band, no bend, no widening. Anything you can see a shape in is the line getting something wrong.

  • On diamonds the band bends: the errors are positive at both ends and negative in the middle, because price rises faster than in a straight line. And it fans out: errors spread about 13 times wider for the big stones than the small ones. A $12,000 prediction on a 2-carat stone is nearly worthless, and R² = 0.84 said nothing about it.
  • On the geyser, an even band, no bend, no fan. Yet look at the scatter itself: two separate blobs. Fit only the short eruptions and R² collapses to 0.08; only the long ones, 0.14. The 0.81 is mostly the gap between two kinds of eruption. It tells you which kind you had; it barely predicts within a kind.
  • On retail orders, the lowest R² and the soundest picture: no bend, and a fan of only about 3× against the diamonds' 13×. Its errors are large but they are honest, and the slope is a number you could plan with — each extra item on an order is worth about £7.

R² is not the useful number. The slope is.

The slope answers "if x goes up by one, what happens to y?" — which is the question a decision actually needs. R² only says how tightly the points hug the line. A small R² with a solid slope is a real, usable finding; a big R² tells you nothing about whether the line's shape is right.

Retail's R² of 0.23 means order size explains under a quarter of why invoices differ. That is not a defect: people buy different things. The £7 per item is still worth knowing, and still worth acting on.

And it says nothing about cause

A line through two columns of numbers does not know which one moved first, or whether something else moved both. Longer eruptions do not cause longer waits — both are driven by how much pressure the geyser built up. Fitting a line is not evidence of a mechanism; it is a description that begs for one.

The same thing, in exam language

ŷ = a + bx
The fitted line. b is the slope, a the intercept.
residual
y − ŷ. One thread on the chart; one dot in the bottom panel.
SST / SSE / SSR
Total, unexplained (error) and explained sums of squares. SST = SSR + SSE — the bar across the middle.
R²
1 − SSE/SST = SSR/SST. For one predictor it also equals r², the squared correlation.
least squares
Choosing a, b to minimise SSE. b = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ−x̄)², a = ȳ − bx̄.
homoskedasticity
Constant error spread — the assumption the diamonds break, visible as the fan in the bottom panel.

Module 8 is this bottom panel with names attached: a bend means the wrong functional form, a fan means heteroskedasticity, and a drift over time means autocorrelation. Learn to read the panel and the diagnostics chapter is mostly vocabulary.
