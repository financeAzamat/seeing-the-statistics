UDJ · Module 9 — Classification & Accuracy

97% accurate and completely useless

The easiest number to game in all of analytics.

Predicting a yes/no outcome invites one number: what share did we get right? It is the most quoted measure in applied machine learning and the easiest one to make look excellent while learning nothing at all.

The trick

Take a real question with a real answer: is this diamond worth more than $10,000? Across all 53,940 stones, 9.68% are. So:

predict "no" for every single diamond → accuracy 90.32%

No model. No data. No thought. Ninety percent. Raise the bar to $15,000 and the same non-answer scores 96.93%. Any model that cannot beat that is worthless, and plenty of reported models cannot.

What the number hides

Accuracy adds up two different successes and two different failures, then throws the distinction away. The four are worth naming, because they have different prices:

  • True positive — flagged, and it was. The point of the exercise.
  • False positive — flagged, but wasn't. Wasted effort; an appraisal, a call, an investigation.
  • False negative — missed. Usually the expensive one: the fraud you paid, the churn you didn't prevent, the defect that shipped.
  • True negative — correctly ignored. Cheap, plentiful, and what inflates accuracy when the event is rare.

The four cells are the honest report. Accuracy is those four numbers with the interesting structure averaged out of them.

Drag the line

The chart is the confusion matrix drawn as geography. The horizontal line is the truth — $10,000, fixed. The vertical line is your decision rule: call everything to its right expensive. Between them they cut the stones into four quadrants, and those quadrants are the four cells.

  1. Drag the vertical line all the way right. You now predict "cheap" for almost everything: accuracy climbs toward 90%, and you catch nothing.
  2. Drag it left. You catch nearly every expensive stone — and flag thousands of cheap ones with it.
  3. Press best accuracy. The best-scoring cut-off still misses 31% of the expensive diamonds.
  4. Press best F1. Accuracy barely moves — 95.07% to 95.03% — but recall jumps from 69% to 84%. Nearly free, and invisible to accuracy.

The two numbers to quote instead

precision = of those you flagged, how many were right recall = of those that mattered, how many did you catch

They pull against each other, and which one you favour is a business decision, not a statistical one. Screening for a cheap follow-up? Favour recall; a false alarm costs a phone call. Triggering something expensive or irreversible? Favour precision.

F1 is their harmonic mean — a single number again, but one that cannot be won by doing nothing, because ignoring every case sends recall to zero and takes F1 with it.

Where this bites

  • Fraud. Under 1% of transactions. A 99% accurate model can be "approve everything".
  • Churn. Predicting nobody leaves scores well and saves no one.
  • Defect screening. Accuracy rewards passing everything; the cost lands with the customer.
  • Any rare event. The rarer the thing, the better doing nothing looks.

Ask for the base rate before the accuracy. Without it the accuracy figure cannot be interpreted at all — and its absence is usually the tell.

In exam language

confusion matrix
The 2×2 of predicted against actual. TP, FP, FN, TN — the four quadrants on screen.
accuracy
(TP+TN)/n. Dominated by TN whenever the positive class is rare.
precision
TP/(TP+FP). Also the positive predictive value.
recall
TP/(TP+FN). Sensitivity, or the true positive rate.
F1
2·precision·recall/(precision+recall). The harmonic mean, so either being near zero drags it down.
base rate
The share of actual positives. The number that decides whether an accuracy figure means anything.
