UDJ · Module 3 — Describing a Distribution

Most people are below average

And that is not an insult. It is arithmetic.

Someone tells you the average order is £519. You picture a typical customer spending about £519. On this real data, that customer barely exists — and more than three in every four orders come in under it.

The number everyone reaches for first

The average is the first thing anyone computes and the first thing anyone quotes. Add everything up, divide by how many. It is the most useful single number in statistics and the easiest one to be misled by, because of a property people rarely think about: the average is not a vote. A single very large value moves it as much as thousands of ordinary ones.

Here is the same three real datasets, and how many observations actually fall below their own average:

Real dataAverageBelow it
Online orders (19,773)£519.50 77.3%
Diamonds (53,940)$3,932.80 63.6%
Geyser waits (272)70.90 min 39.3%

On the orders, 15,285 of 19,773 are below the average. Quote £519.50 as the typical order and you have described a customer that 77% of them are not.

Why the first two behave that way

Order values and diamond prices have the same shape: a great many small ones, then a long thin tail of very large ones running off to the right. That tail cannot pull the average down — there is nothing below zero — so it only ever pulls it up, away from the crowd.

a few huge values drag the average up the crowd stays where it was → most of the crowd is now "below average"

The fix is the one statistic almost nobody quotes: the median, the value with half the observations either side. It is a vote. One giant order counts once, like everyone else.

Real dataAverageMiddle valueGap
Online orders£519.50£302.59 1.72×
Diamonds$3,932.80$2,401.00 1.64×

The middle order is £302.59. That is the one to picture. The average is 72% higher than it, and the difference is entirely a handful of very large orders.

The third dataset breaks the rule — and that is the real lesson

Only 39.3% of geyser waits are below their average, so "most are below average" is simply false there. But the average fails on that data too, in a completely different way: it lands on a value that hardly ever happens.

Old Faithful has two habits. Short waits cluster around 52.9 minutes and long waits around 79.4 minutes. The average of the two habits is 70.90 minutes — a wait that belongs to neither. Only 7.0% of waits land within a couple of minutes of it, against 16.9% at the busier cluster.

Turn up at the average time and you have picked the emptiest part of the schedule. There is no such thing as a typical wait here, and no single number can invent one.

How often does the average actually happen?

Ask that of all three and the average looks worse still. Counting how many observations land in a narrow window around the average, versus the same window around the commonest value:

Real dataNear the averageNear the commonest
Online orders14.4%37.1%
Diamonds7.0%31.2%
Geyser waits7.0%16.9%

The commonest diamond price is about $965. The average is $3,932.80 — more than four times higher. Both numbers are correct. Only one of them describes a diamond you are likely to see.

Try it yourself

Switch datasets and watch where the two markers sit. The bars below the average are shaded; the counter tells you how much of the data that is. Then switch to the geyser and watch the average fall into the gap between the two humps.

Where this appears in practice

You already rely on this and may not have noticed:

  • Salaries. Average pay at a company is pulled up by a few senior packages. The median is what a new joiner should ask about.
  • House prices. Property reporting quotes medians almost exclusively, precisely because a handful of mansions would wreck the mean.
  • Page load times. Engineers watch the median and the 95th percentile, never the average, because the average hides both the normal case and the disaster.
  • Capacity planning. Sizing anything for the average demand guarantees you are short whenever demand is above it — which, on right-skewed demand, is the busy periods that matter.

Before you quote an average, ask what shape the data is. If it has a tail, quote the median instead. If it has two humps, quote both and stop pretending there is a typical case.

In exam language

The arithmetic mean is not a robust measure of central tendency: its influence function is unbounded, so a single extreme observation has unlimited leverage. The median is the 50th percentile and has a breakdown point of 50%.

For a right-skewed (positively skewed) distribution, mean > median > mode, and the proportion below the mean exceeds one half. The measured skewness is 3.40 for order value and 1.61 for diamond price.

The geyser waits are bimodal. Central tendency is not well defined for a multimodal distribution; the mean falls in the antimode. Reporting a single measure of location here is a specification error, not a rounding problem. Formally this data is a mixture distribution, and the correct summary describes the components.

Quoting mean and standard deviation implicitly asserts approximate normality. Both of the skewed sets fail that assertion, which is why Module 5 needs a sample size of 290 rather than 30 before the average of order values behaves.

Three real datasets, no simulation. Order values are every completed invoice from a UK online gift retailer over one year; diamonds are the full 53,940-stone reference table; waits are 272 consecutive Old Faithful eruptions. Every figure quoted above is re-derived from the raw sources by verify_typical.py, and the two-cluster claim is only made where a measured test for genuine bimodality passes.
