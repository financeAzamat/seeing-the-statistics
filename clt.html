The Central Limit Theorem

How looking at 30 customers tells you something dependable about 20,000.

One customer is unpredictable. The next order might be £5 or £4,000 — you cannot plan on it. Thirty customers, averaged, are almost boring: that average lands near £458 nearly every time you take it.

Individual things are chaotic. Averages of groups are dependable. That swap is the whole trick, and almost everything you have ever been told about "what people do" rests on it.

What you are looking at

The pile on top is real data: 700 actual invoices from a real UK online shop. Each dot is one order, sitting where its value falls — cheap orders to the left, expensive ones to the right. They run from about £2 to just over £4,000, and they are bunched up hard on the left, because most orders are small and only a few are big.

Now watch. Thirty of those dots light up: picked at random, like pulling receipts out of a box. Their values get averaged into a single number, and that number drops as one dot into the pile at the bottom. Then it happens again. And again.

The bottom pile is the thing to watch. It is narrow, it sits in the middle, and it is shaped like a bell — even though the pile it came from is nothing like a bell.

Why that is strange

Compare the two piles side by side. The orders themselves are spread across the whole width of the screen. The averages of thirty of them barely move — they huddle in a narrow band around the middle.

Nothing was cleaned up or smoothed to make that happen. Same data, same axis. The only thing that changed is that you stopped asking about one order and started asking about the average of thirty.

Try it yourself

  1. Click n = 1. The bottom pile turns into a copy of the top one. An "average" of a single order is just that order — no magic yet.
  2. Click n = 30. The bottom pile collapses into a narrow bell. That collapse is the theorem doing its work.
  3. Click 25, then 100. The typical miss halves, £118 → £59. Four times the data bought exactly twice the precision — and never more than that.
  4. Switch to geyser. That data has two separate humps and nothing in the middle. The averages still come out as one clean bell.

Why you already rely on this

You have trusted this result many times without being told its name:

  • Every poll you have seen. "±3 points" from asking 1,000 people about an electorate of tens of millions — that number comes straight out of this.
  • Every medicine you have taken. Approved on a trial of a few thousand people, not on the population that will end up swallowing it.
  • Every website you use. The layout was chosen by testing two versions on a sample of visitors and comparing the averages.
  • Every food safety check. A handful of items per batch get tested, and the batch gets cleared or pulled.
  • Every forecast your company signs off. Built from a sample, presented with a range around it.

None of that works unless the average of a sample behaves predictably. This page is the demonstration that it does.

The one rule worth remembering

typical miss = spread of the data ÷ √(how much data)

The square root is the part with consequences. To halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. To cut it to a tenth, a hundred times.

That is why polls ask 1,000 people and not 10,000: paying ten times more buys only a threefold improvement. It is also the reason "we asked 12 customers" deserves suspicion while "we asked 400" usually does not — and why, past a point, collecting more data is the expensive way to solve a problem.

Where it stops working

You will be told n ≈ 30 is enough. That is a rule about how extreme your data's tail is, and you can watch it fail right here. The exact version:

lopsidedness of the averages = lopsidedness of the data ÷ √n

Take 0.20 as the point a pile stops looking lopsided to the eye. The three datasets then need wildly different amounts of data, and only one of them is served by 30:

  • Geyser waits — mildly lopsided (−0.42) → even by n = 5.
  • Diamond prices — strongly (+1.61) → needs n = 66.
  • Retail orders — extremely (+3.40) → needs n ≈ 290. At 30 the averages still lean right, and you can see them doing it.

The wilder the tail, the more data you need before averages start behaving. Thirty is a starting guess, not a guarantee. Watch the two lopsidedness readings as you change n — the live one tracks the prediction to about two decimal places.

The same thing, in exam language

population
Everything you care about — here, all 19,583 invoices.
sample (n)
The handful you actually drew. The dots that light up.
x̄ (sample mean)
The average of one sample. One gold dot.
μ, σ
The population's true mean and spread. The gold line, and the width of the top pile.
sampling distribution of the mean
The bottom pile. Not the data — the distribution of averages of the data.
standard error (SE)
The width of the bottom pile, σ/√n. The "typical miss" above.

Both of the formulas you will be examined on are statements about that bottom pile's width:

CI:   x̄ ± z · s/√n Test: z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

Learn the pile and neither one is a formula to memorise.
