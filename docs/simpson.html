UDJ · Extra — Simpson's Paradox

When the average points the wrong way

Not misleading. Backwards.

Every number so far has hidden something. This one does worse: it gives you the opposite of the truth, and it does so on real diamonds, in a dataset a jeweller would recognise.

The claim the data appears to make

Diamonds are graded for colour on a letter scale — J is the worst, D the best. Average the price of every stone at each grade and you get this:

Colour gradeMean priceMean size
J (worst)$5,3241.162 ct
D (best)$3,1700.658 ct

Read literally: the worst colour costs 68% more than the best. Buy the ugliest stones, they're the valuable ones.

Now hold the size still

The right-hand column is the tell. Top-colour stones are little — barely half the weight. Compare like with like and the ordering snaps back:

Size bandJ (worst)D (best)
0.2–0.5 ct$596$846
0.5–0.8 ct$1,643$2,335
0.8–1.1 ct$3,404$5,591
1.1–1.6 ct$6,030$9,387
1.6–5.1 ct$12,436$14,154

In every single size band, the better colour costs more. Five out of five. The aggregate says the exact opposite of all five groups it is made of.

Why it happens

Size dominates price, and size is tangled up with grade. A big rough stone is rarely cut down to chase top colour — the weight is worth more than the letter. So the top grades fill up with small stones, and the average price follows the size rather than the grade.

the confound: grade → size → price ↘_______↗

Average over everything and you measure the wrong arrow. The paradox is not a statistical curiosity; it is what happens when you average across a variable that is doing the real work.

Try it yourself

  1. Leave the band on all sizes. The price bars slope down as the grade improves. That is the aggregate, and it is backwards.
  2. Click any single size band. Watch the bars flip and climb. Same data, same grades — one variable held still.
  3. Look at the size panel while you do it. That downward slope is the whole mechanism, sitting in plain view.
  4. Switch to cut and clarity. Both reverse too — 4 of 5 bands and 3 of 3. It is not a quirk of one grading.

Where this bites in real work

  • "Our cheapest channel has the best margin." It may just sell the easiest products.
  • "Treatment A has better outcomes." If A was given to healthier patients, the aggregate lies in exactly this shape — the original Simpson's example was medical.
  • "This region underperforms." Check the mix before the verdict; region and product are rarely independent.
  • "Hiring rates differ by department." The famous Berkeley admissions case is this table with different labels.

Any average taken across a mixture is a claim about the mixture as much as about the thing you meant to measure. Before quoting one, ask what else changes between the groups.

In exam language

confounder
A variable related to both the grouping and the outcome. Here: carat.
marginal vs conditional
The aggregate mean is marginal — averaged over carat. The band means are conditional on carat. They can disagree in direction, and that disagreement is Simpson's paradox.
stratification
Splitting into bands before comparing. The cheapest fix, and the one this page performs.
why regression helps
Putting carat in the model does the same job continuously: the grade coefficient is then "holding size constant", which is what the band tables show one slice at a time.

This is also why Module 7's multiple regression exists at all. A slope with one predictor answers a marginal question; the interesting business questions are nearly always conditional ones.
