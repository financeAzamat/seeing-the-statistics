UDJ · Module 6 — Hypothesis Tests & p-values

The 5% you cannot avoid

How to "discover" something that was never there — and miss something that was.

You run an A/B test. Version B comes out ahead. Before you ship it, one question has to be answered: could a gap that size have happened by luck alone? That question is the whole of hypothesis testing, and the p-value is the answer to it.

What you are looking at

Two samples are drawn from the same real dataset. Group A is left alone. Group B has the "change" applied to it — a lift you choose, which might be nothing at all. Both averages are worked out, and the gap between them gets a p-value.

Every test drops one bar into the histogram at the bottom. Careful: that bottom chart is on a completely different scale from the data above it. It does not run in pounds — it runs from p = 0 on the left to p = 1 on the right.

What a p-value actually is

p = if nothing were really going on, how often would luck alone hand me a gap at least this big?

That is all it is. A small p means your gap is hard to explain by luck. A large p means luck explains it comfortably. The cut-off you compare it to — usually 0.05 — is a convention someone picked, not a law.

What it is not

A p-value is not the probability that nothing is going on. It runs the other way round: it assumes nothing is going on, then measures how surprising your data would be. "p = 0.03" does not mean a 3% chance you are wrong.

The flat histogram, and where the 5% comes from

Set the change to none — the two groups are now genuinely identical — and watch the bottom chart fill in. It comes out flat. Every p-value from 0 to 1 is equally likely, which is a fact worth sitting with, because it has a consequence:

If one twentieth of the width sits below 0.05, then one test in twenty clears the bar when nothing is happening. The 5% false-alarm rate is not a mistake anyone made. It is the definition, working as intended.

Measured on this data with nothing going on: 4.7% of tests come out "significant" at the 0.05 line. Exactly as advertised — and exactly the problem.

Which is why 20 tests find a winner

Test one thing and you have a 1-in-20 chance of a false alarm. Test twenty things and the chances of at least one are:

So "we tested a lot of segments and found one that was significant" is not a finding. It is the expected behaviour of a method being run twenty times. Always ask how many things were tested before the winner was reported.

The bigger problem nobody mentions

False alarms get all the attention. The opposite failure is far more common and much more expensive. Set a real +5% lift and see how often the test actually catches it — that share is called the power:

On retail order value, a real 5% lift with 2,000 customers in each group is caught 23% of the time. You would miss it three times out of four, and report "no significant difference" every time.

So "not significant" almost never means "no effect". Usually it means the test was never capable of seeing an effect that size. A negative result without a power figure beside it carries almost no information.

Why retail is so much harder than the geyser

One number explains the whole table: how big the noise is relative to the average.

retail orders spread ÷ average = 1.28 diamond prices spread ÷ average = 1.00 geyser waits spread ÷ average = 0.19

Retail's noise is about 6.7 times larger relative to its average than the geyser's. Because required sample size scales with the square of that ratio, retail needs roughly 45 times as much data to see the same percentage lift. Skewed money data is expensive to test on, and that cost is knowable in advance.

What to do about it

  • Work out the power before you run the test, not after. A test that cannot detect the effect you care about is not worth running.
  • Decide what size of effect matters first. "Any improvement" is not a target, and it makes the sample size infinite.
  • Count and declare how many things you tested. One winner out of twenty comparisons is a coin landing heads, not a discovery.
  • Never read "not significant" as "no difference." Report the interval alongside it so the reader can see what was still possible.
  • Reduce the noise instead of buying more data where you can — pair before and after on the same customers, or test on a less erratic metric.

The same thing, in exam language

H₀ / HA
Null: the two means are equal. Alternative: they are not. The test only ever assumes H₀ and measures surprise.
p-value
P(data this extreme | H₀ true). Note the direction of the bar — it is not P(H₀ | data).
α (significance level)
The false-positive rate you accept in advance. The red zone on the histogram.
Type I / Type II error
Crying wolf (rate α) / missing a real effect (rate β). Power = 1 − β, the right-hand tables above.
Welch two-sample t
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂), with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Used here rather than the pooled version because scaling group B scales its spread too.

The link back to Module 5 is exact, not an analogy:

CI: x̄ ± t · s/√n Test: t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

A value falls outside the 95% interval exactly when the two-sided test rejects at 5%. One procedure, two presentations.

Every p-value on screen is computed with a real t distribution, not a normal approximation: stats.js implements the incomplete beta function and is checked against scipy to better than 1e-10 by verify_stats_js.py, including the far tail where the naive 1 − cdf would return zero.
