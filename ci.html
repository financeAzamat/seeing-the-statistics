UDJ · Module 5 — Confidence Intervals

What "95% confident" actually buys you

And why, on real business data, it is often not 95%.

In real life you get one sample. You never see the true average, so you report a range and attach a number to it: "the average order is £430 to £490, 95% confident." This page shows you exactly what that sentence promises — and then shows it failing on real data.

What you are looking at

Same real data as before, in the pile at the top. A sample of orders is picked, its average is worked out, and a bar is drawn around that average — the range you would have reported if that had been your one sample.

The gold line running down the screen is the true average, the thing you are trying to catch. In real life you cannot see it. Here you can, which is the only reason we can grade the answers.

Bars that catch the gold line stay blue. Bars that miss it turn red. Then it happens again, and the bars stack up.

The thing almost everyone gets wrong

"95% confident" is not a statement about your interval. It is a statement about the method: intervals built this way catch the true average 95% of the time. Yours either caught it or it did not, and you will never find out which.

Look at any single blue bar on screen. Nothing about it tells you it is one of the good ones — the red bars look exactly the same from the inside. That is the honest position you are always in with real data: you have one bar, no gold line, and a track record for the method.

Try it yourself

  1. Leave it running on 95% and watch the counter. Roughly 1 bar in 20 turns red — that is the promise being kept.
  2. Drop to 80%. Bars get shorter, and far more of them turn red. Narrow ranges are not better answers; they are bolder bets.
  3. Go to 99%. Almost nothing misses — because the range is now so wide it is barely a claim at all. That is the trade in full view.
  4. Switch to the sample's own spread. This is what you actually do at work, because nobody hands you the true spread. Watch what happens to the number.

Where the promise breaks

Step 4 is the point of this page. There are two versions of the calculation:

σ known: average ± z × σ / √n σ estimated: average ± t × s / √n

The first assumes you already know how spread out the whole population is. You never do. The second uses the spread of your own sample, which is the honest version and the one you were taught to use.

On the retail order data, asking for 95% and estimating the spread from a sample of 30 gets you 86%. One in seven of your "95% confident" statements is simply wrong.

Why it breaks, in one sentence

Most orders are small and a few are huge. Draw 30 orders and miss the big ones, and two things go wrong at the same time: your average comes out too low, and your spread comes out too small — so you build a narrow range in the wrong place.

Confidently wrong, and always in the same direction. On retail orders at n = 30 the misses split 13.6% too low against 0.2% too high. The mean and the spread of a sample move together on skewed data — their correlation is +0.87.

A confidence interval on skewed money data is not just unreliable, it is biased. It understates. If you have ever been surprised by costs coming in above a forecast range, this is one of the mechanisms.

What to do about it

  • Get more data than the rule of thumb says. The same retail interval reaches 94.5% at n = 300. Skew is paid for in sample size.
  • Check the shape before trusting the range. Mean far above median is the warning sign, and it takes one line to check.
  • Expect the miss to be on the low side, and say so when presenting the range. A stated asymmetry is worth more than a false symmetry.
  • Prefer methods that do not assume symmetry: bootstrap intervals, or work on a log scale — when the money is skewed and the decision is real.

The same thing, in exam language

confidence interval
One bar. x̄ ± (critical value) × SE.
confidence level
The long-run share of such intervals that contain μ — the coverage, not a probability about your one bar.
z vs t
z when σ is known; t with n−1 degrees of freedom when you estimate it with s. t is wider, and the gap shrinks as n grows.
margin of error
Half the bar's width.
coverage
What the method actually delivers. Equals the confidence level only when the assumptions hold — which on skewed data at small n, they do not.

Both the interval and the Module 6 test statistic are built from the same standard error:

CI: x̄ ± t · s/√n Test: t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)

A value falls outside the 95% interval exactly when the two-sided test rejects at 5%. They are one procedure written two ways — which also means the coverage problem above is a false-positive-rate problem in Module 6.

Coverage on screen is counted from the intervals actually drawn, so it wobbles early and settles as the count climbs. The critical values are generated into ci_crit.js by the measurement script and checked against published tables, rather than approximated in the browser.
