UDJ · Extra — Simpson's Paradox

Every size band contradicts the average

The aggregate reverses the direction all five groups agree on.

The diamonds reference table holds 53,940 stones, each carrying a colour grade from J to D and a retail price. Average the price at each grade and the worst colour comes out $2,154 above the best. Hold weight still and the ordering reverses in all five size bands. Both results are computed from the same 53,940 records.

The claim the data appears to make

Colour is graded on a letter scale, J worst to D best. Averaging the price of every stone within each grade gives this:

Colour gradeMean priceMean size
J (worst)$5,3241.162 ct
D (best)$3,1700.658 ct

Read as a statement about colour, that says the worst colour costs 68% more than the best.

Now hold the size still

The mean size column carries the explanation. Stones graded D average 0.658 carats; stones graded J average 1.162 carats, nearly twice the weight. The two groups are not comparable on the variable that sets price. Comparing within a single size band removes that difference:

In all five size bands the better colour costs more. The aggregate reverses the direction every one of its own groups agrees on.

Why it happens

Weight sets price, and weight is entangled with grade. A large rough stone is rarely cut down to reach a higher colour, because the carats are worth more than the letter. The top grades therefore fill with small stones, and the average price tracks weight rather than colour. Averaging over the whole table measures weight while appearing to measure colour.

the confound: grade → size → price ↘_______↗

This happens whenever an average is taken across a variable that is doing the work.

Try it yourself

  1. Leave the band on all sizes. The price bars slope down as the grade improves. That is the aggregate.
  2. Select any single size band. The bars flip and climb. Same stones, same grades, one variable held still.
  3. Watch the size panel while you do it. Its downward slope is the mechanism.
  4. Switch to cut and clarity. Both reverse too — 4 of 5 bands and 3 of 3. The effect is not a quirk of one grading.

Where this appears in practice

  • A channel with the best margin may simply sell the easiest products.
  • A treatment with better outcomes may have been given to healthier patients. Simpson's original example was medical.
  • A region that appears to underperform may carry a different product mix. Region and product are rarely independent.
  • Admission rates that differ by department are this same table with different labels. That is the Berkeley case.

An average across a mixture is a claim about the mixture as much as about the thing being measured. Ask what else differs between the groups before quoting one.

In exam language

confounder
A variable related to both the grouping and the outcome. Here: carat.
marginal vs conditional
The aggregate mean is marginal — averaged over carat. The band means are conditional on carat. They can disagree in direction, and that disagreement is Simpson's paradox.
stratification
Splitting into bands before comparing. The cheapest fix, and the one this page performs.
why regression helps
Putting carat in the model does the same job continuously: the grade coefficient is then "holding size constant", which is what the band tables show one slice at a time.

This is also why Module 7's multiple regression exists at all. A slope with one predictor answers a marginal question; the interesting business questions are nearly always conditional ones.
